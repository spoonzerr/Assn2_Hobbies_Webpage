Welcome to
Gaming

Gaming looks like a closed room — the gear, the hours, the reflexes. It isn't. Most nights it's whoever happened to be free, and there's always room for more.

How it all began

It started with whatever was free and whoever was online. It stuck because the games kept changing and the people on the calls didn't.

Why I play

Games are such an easy way to spend time with people. Being spread across different schedules and different parts of the world, a game lobby is the one place we all reliably turn up.

The games themselves matter less than they look like they do. Some nights it's competitive and everyone wants to win; some nights it's whatever went free that week and everyone's just trying to have fun.

Half the lobby is trying to win. The other half is just having fun. Nobody's playing the wrong way.

Advantages that come with games

Gaming gets defended badly — usually with studies nobody's read. These are the things I'd actually stand behind, from my experiences.

Friendships usually end quietly. People move, schedules stop lining up, and nobody actually decides to stop talking. Online games take the effort out of staying in touch — even without seeing each other in person, a shared call is enough to stay close.

A single round comes down to a few hundred small decisions, all made on incomplete information with no time to think them through. You get used to picking a read and committing to it, which turns out to be useful well outside the game.

Five people, one objective, and no way to carry it alone. You learn to pass on information clearly, trust what someone else is telling you, and own a mistake without it turning into an argument. Group projects would go better if everyone had played a season of it.

Two people, five, or a dozen — there's usually something that fits. Too many for one lobby? Switch games. There's no such thing as having too many people.

Working out why something keeps failing, trying a fix, then fixing the fix. It's the same loop as debugging, except you're doing it voluntarily at midnight.

Losing costs nothing here, so it's an easy place to be new at something. That gets harder to practise as you get older, and it's worth more than any rank.

Most ways of relaxing leave room to keep worrying in the background. A match doesn't — it takes enough attention that there's nothing left over. An hour in, whatever you were stuck on has usually loosened.
